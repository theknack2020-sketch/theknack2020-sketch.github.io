Septuagint LXX: Old Testament

Version 2.0.0 · November 17, 2026 · from TheKnack

Septuagint LXX: Old Testament is an iPhone and iPad app for reading the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament that the New Testament writers often quoted, in Sir Lancelot Brenton’s 1851 English translation, with his Greek text beside it. Its Apostles’ Map holds 1,606 links between the New Testament and the Old: 304 quotations, each labeled Follows the Greek, Follows the Hebrew or Neither exactly, and 1,302 allusions. The English text of all 52 books, search and the whole Apostles’ Map work without Septuagint Pro, which adds the Greek text in every chapter, map insights, every reading plan, longer book introductions and unlimited bookmarks, highlights and notes.

Who it is for

  • Readers who want the Old Testament as the Septuagint has it, in English, with the deuterocanonical books in their places in the canon rather than in an appendix.
  • Readers of Brenton’s diglot who want his Greek and English together, verse by verse.
  • Students of the New Testament who want to see which Old Testament verse a passage quotes or alludes to, and whose wording a quotation follows.
  • Orthodox readers who count the psalms the Greek way and want the Hebrew numbers beside them.

What it is not

  • It is not a whole Bible: it holds the Old Testament, and New Testament verses appear only in the Apostles’ Map, in the King James Version.
  • It is not a critical Greek edition or an interlinear: the Greek is Brenton’s own text, set verse by verse beside his English, with no Hebrew text and no word-by-word glosses.
  • It has no commentary and no study notes from a publisher; the book introductions are written for this app.
  • Search reads the English text only, and Brenton’s Greek has no text for 1 Kingdoms 17:12–31, so those 20 verses are in English only.

What it does

Read

  • Brenton’s English text of 52 books in four sections, Law, History, Poetry and Prophets: 28,616 verses in 1,103 chapters.
  • Brenton’s Greek text, 28,598 verses, shown instead of the English or with it; where the page is wide enough, Greek and English stand in facing columns.
  • Every verse Brenton numbers with a letter is in the text, 307 in all, among them the 88 lettered verses of Greek Esther that hold the Additions to Esther.
  • Psalms carry both numbers: your Psalm 23 is Psalm 22 here, with Hebrew 23 beside it, and Psalm 151 is included.
  • Where the Greek orders things differently, the page says so: Jeremiah shows the Hebrew chapter number and a table of how the chapters line up, Proverbs 30 says its material is in chapter 24, and psalms the Greek joins or splits carry a note.
  • Nehemiah is printed once, as 2 Esdras 11–23, where the Greek canon has it; the Library still lists it by name, and each of those chapters shows its Nehemiah number.
  • Four page colors (Auto, Paper, Sepia and Night), adjustable text size and three line spacings.

The Apostles’ Map

  • 1,606 links join 1,029 New Testament verses to 1,149 Old Testament verses.
  • 304 links are quotations, where the New Testament reproduces the wording of the verse; 1,302 are allusions, which point back without quoting and make no claim about whose wording they follow.
  • Of the 304 quotations, 268 follow the Greek, 8 follow the Hebrew and 28 match neither exactly.
  • Hebrews 10:5 is the clearest case: the Hebrew of Psalm 40:6 reads “mine ears hast thou opened”, the Greek reads “a body hast thou prepared me”, and Hebrews quotes the Greek.
  • The map is drawn as a weaving of threads you can touch and listed book by book from Matthew to Revelation, with filters for quotations, allusions and each label.
  • It works in both directions: tapping a marked verse in the reader lists every passage that draws on it, and each link’s page lists the other passages that draw on the same verse.
  • Map insights count the links by New Testament book, by writer and by Old Testament book, and list the verses quoted most; without Septuagint Pro each list shows its first 3 rows.

Search and find

  • Search takes a reference such as Isaiah 53, a book by its Greek or Hebrew-order name (3 Kingdoms 19 or 1 Kings 19), or any word or phrase in the English text.
  • A psalm number is read both ways: Psalm 23 finds Greek Psalm 23 and Hebrew Psalm 23, which is Psalm 22 here, and Hebrew Psalms 9, 10, 114 and 115 are found inside the Greek psalms that join them.
  • Books and chapters appear in the device’s own search, psalms under both numbers.
  • Siri and Shortcuts can continue your reading, open a book, or open a psalm by its Greek or Hebrew number.

Marks, plans and introductions

  • Bookmarks, highlights and notes: notes and bookmark labels can be edited, highlights can be removed, and every deletion can be undone.
  • Everything you mark exports as one plain-text file.
  • Any verse can be copied, shared as text or shared as a picture card, and any link of the map can be shared as a picture card with its source.
  • Every book has a short introduction, followed by a longer one whose opening every reader sees.
  • Five reading plans: What the apostles quoted (ten books, five chapters a day for 75 days), the canon in a year, the Psalter by kathisma, the Psalter in a month, and Genesis, Isaiah and Proverbs in forty days.

On iPad

  • In a wide window the list of books stands in a column beside the page, and it can be put away to give the page the whole width.
  • The app runs in Split View, Slide Over and Stage Manager, and a narrow window gets the phone layout.
  • With a keyboard, Command-1 to Command-4 switch tabs, Command-F searches the text, and Command with the left or right arrow turns the chapter.

How it works

  • The links start from the openbible.info cross-reference list (CC BY), selected and measured for this app, and the app credits that source and its license in Settings.
  • A link counts as a quotation only when the New Testament reproduces the wording of the verse, a clause or more: 255 quotations were found by measuring the wording, 20 are marked in Brenton’s own edition, and 29 were decided by hand, which their page says.
  • The Greek label comes from comparing the Greek New Testament (Textus Receptus) with Brenton’s Greek; for the Hebrew side, the King James Old Testament in English stands in for the Hebrew.
  • “Neither exactly” is on 28 of the 304 quotations; the app explains it as often a quotation from memory, a paraphrase or a blend of both, and shows no reason for single links.
  • The Greek and Hebrew traditions divide the 150 psalms differently: 133 Greek psalm numbers are one behind the Hebrew, 11 are the same, and 6 Greek psalms join or split Hebrew ones.

Privacy

No account, no ads, no analytics and no tracking. No permissions of any kind: the app asks for no camera, photos, notifications, location, contacts or microphone. The app’s own code makes no network calls; buying Septuagint Pro goes through Apple, and web pages open only when you tap a link. Your bookmarks, highlights, notes and reading progress are stored on your device and are part of your own device backup if you keep one, and the device search holds only the names and opening words of books and chapters.

Questions

Is there an app that shows where the New Testament quotes the Septuagint?

Yes. The Apostles’ Map in Septuagint LXX: Old Testament holds 1,606 links between New Testament verses and the Old Testament verses they draw on, and tells the 304 quotations apart from the 1,302 allusions. Each quotation is labeled Follows the Greek (268), Follows the Hebrew (8) or Neither exactly (28), and the whole map, in both directions, works without Septuagint Pro.

Is there a Brenton Septuagint app for iPhone and iPad?

Yes. Septuagint LXX: Old Testament has Sir Lancelot Brenton’s 1851 English translation of all 52 books, and his Greek text, on iPhone and iPad. The whole English text can be read without Septuagint Pro.

Does it show the Greek text beside the English?

Yes. The page can show English, Greek or both, and where the page is wide enough Greek and English stand in facing columns. Without Septuagint Pro, chapter 1 of every book has the Greek in full and every other chapter shows the Greek of its first 3 verses; Septuagint Pro opens the Greek of every chapter.

Does it include the deuterocanonical books, the Additions to Esther and Psalm 151?

Yes. Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, the Letter of Jeremiah, 1 to 4 Maccabees, 1 Esdras, the Prayer of Manasseh, Susanna and Bel and the Dragon stand in their places in the canon, and Psalm 151 is included. Greek Esther has all 252 of its verses, including the 88 lettered verses that hold the Additions, and Greek Daniel 3 includes the Prayer of Azarias and the song of the three young men.

Why is Psalm 23 numbered 22?

Because the Greek and Hebrew traditions divide the psalms differently, most Greek psalm numbers are one behind the Hebrew. The app prints the Hebrew number beside the Greek one, and Search, Siri and Shortcuts find a psalm by either number.

Is it an Orthodox Old Testament?

It is Brenton’s 1851 English translation of the Septuagint, with his Greek text, in the order and numbering of the Greek Bible: the app marks 3 Maccabees and the Prayer of Manasseh as read in the Orthodox canon and 4 Maccabees as an appendix in the Bible of the Greek Church, and it includes Psalm 151. It is not an edition published by a church, and it has no commentary.

Does it work offline, and does it have ads?

It works offline and has no ads: the English and Greek text and the map are inside the app, and its own code makes no network calls. It needs no account and asks for no permissions.

What does Septuagint Pro add?

Brenton’s Greek beside the English in every chapter, map insights in full, every reading plan, the longer introduction to each book in full, and unlimited bookmarks, highlights and notes (25 of each are included). The English text of every book, search and the whole Apostles’ Map work without it.
